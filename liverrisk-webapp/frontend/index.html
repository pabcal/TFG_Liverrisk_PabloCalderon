LiverRisk

A machine learning tool for ranking MASLD patient risk.

LiverRisk ranks MASLD (fatty liver disease) patients by estimated risk of a major hepatic event or death, using a blend of three survival models trained on a heavily censored dataset filled with missing values. It produces a relative ranking within a reference population based on risk. Rank 1 being the highest risk patient.

Behind the Scenes

See what's actually driving these predictions. The data, the models, and how well it all holds up.

The data

  • Real, longitudinal hospital records: patients tracked over up to 22 separate clinic visits across as long as two decades, with 12 different labs and clinical measures recorded at each visit. This resulted in over 287 features, with most of them being empty if patients haven't taken that test
  • Two outcomes, both genuinely rare: right censoring (patients who leave the study before anything happens) affects the majority of the cohort, requiring survival analysis methods rather than standard classification:
    • A major hepatic event occurs in just 3.75% of patients.
    • Death (of known status) occurs in 7.7% of patients.
  • Synthetic, privacy preserving data: built by a French national research center (ANNITIA, led by IHU ICAN at Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital, Paris) from real patient records collected between 2001 and 2025. Patient data was regenerated to preserve the real clinical patterns and relationships in the data, without exposing any individual's actual medical record.
    • Non invasive fibrosis tests (FibroTest, AIx-P, FibroScan-type liver stiffness) alongside standard labs, not just basic bloodwork.

Feature engineering: turning 22 censored, gap-filled visits into 255 features

This wasn't clean data. The data was so censored that roughly 21.5% of patients had entirely unknown death status. Two of the three fibrosis tests were missing for 35% of patients. Only 47 of 1,253 patients (3.75%) ever had a hepatic event, meaning the other 96% carry no confirmed outcome at all, just "nothing happened yet, as far as we know." None of this could be dropped or silently filled in without throwing away most of the dataset's real signal.

  • Every lab collapsed into 12+ engineered signals per marker. Not just a snapshot value, but first, last, minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, missingness rate, and a regression fitted slope per year, capturing whether a patient's liver markers are trending up or down, not just where they stand today.
  • Missingness turned into a feature, not a gap. Instead of guessing at a missing value, the model is told directly how much of a patient's record is incomplete, so it can weigh these records differently from dense ones.
  • Censoring handled properly, not discarded. Patients who never had an event during observation aren't thrown out. Survival analysis methods let every patient's partial information contribute, right up to the point they left the study.
  • Clinical formulas reproduced. FIB-4 and APRI computed directly from raw labs, both at baseline and at the most recent visit, so the model can be benchmarked against exactly what a hepatologist already uses.

About 15 raw lab measurements, each recorded at up to 22 visits per patient, were transformed into 255 features — over 15 times more usable information than the raw data alone. The goal wasn't just more numbers: each feature was built to capture how a patient's labs change over time, and how complete or incomplete their record is.

Why this had to be survival analysis, not standard prediction

  • Patients are followed for wildly different lengths of time: some for months, some for two decades, and most never experience the tracked event during observation.
  • A standard classifier would have to either discard every patient without a confirmed outcome, or mislabel “haven't had the event yet” as “never will” — both approaches would throw away most of the dataset's real signal.

Three fundamentally different models, independently tuned

  • Coxnet (penalized Cox regression), Random Survival Forest (500 trees), and XGBoost (gradient boosted survival trees): three structurally unrelated approaches, deliberately combined so no single model's blind spots dominate the final score.
  • Every model's hyperparameters were tuned independently. A cross validated search discovered, for example, that a heavily regularized linear model works best for the rare hepatic event endpoint, while a much less conservative configuration wins for mortality.
  • Rank-based blending, not simple averaging. Each model's predictions are converted to percentiles before being combined, so a model with a wider numeric scale can never silently dominate the ensemble.

The blend weighting itself was also discovered through a grid search over every valid weight combination. The findings were that the strongest hepatic event predictions actually come from the forest model alone, while mortality benefits from a three way blend.

How well it actually works, using the C-Index
0.849
Weighted concordance index (0.7 × hepatic + 0.3 × death)
  • Weighted concordance index: 0.849 — the official metric set by ANNITIA and IHU ICAN (70% hepatic event ranking accuracy, 30% mortality), evaluated with cross validation so every patient is scored only by a model that never saw them during training.
  • Benchmarked against real clinical formulas, not just “ the ML score is higher.” A bootstrap resampling analysis (1,000 resamples) was used to test whether the model's advantage over FIB-4 and APRI is actually real and not a result of chance.
